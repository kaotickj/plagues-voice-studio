It started with a leak in the old water purifier at the back of the Rexford lot. Not a big one, just a slow drip from the copper coil near the foundation. The kind of thing you’d expect after a decade of using rusted filters and boiling rainwater to keep the pipes from freezing. But when the leak hit the sump pump, it flooded the storage room where Elias kept his mutfruit stock. That was bad because mutfruit’s not just food—it’s the only thing that keeps the soil alive in this patch of Boston, and without it, the patch dies. The soil turns to dust, the roots wither, and soon the whole thing becomes a ghost of what it once was.

Elias didn’t have much to lose, really. He’d been a botanist before the war, used to growing things in cracked terrariums and old greenhouses. Now he just tended the mutfruit rows behind the Rexford, tending to them with a cracked trowel and a voice that sounded like it had been through too many winters. He’d been quiet for months, mostly because the water purifier kept failing and the city had stopped sending replacement parts. But when the leak came, he didn’t just fix it—he tried to get a hold of a salvaged water pump from the Memory Den. Said it was old, used in the old days to filter rainwater through layers of charcoal and sand. He didn’t know if it’d work, but he needed it to stop the flood before the roots went black.

That’s when he met Lila, a young woman with a satchel full of caps and a pack brahmin that walked beside her like it had a mind of its own. She was passing through on a scavenging run, heading to the old train yard to pick up a rusted generator. Elias asked if she’d seen any signs of the water purifier’s previous owner. “No,” she said, “but I saw a note tucked under the door. Said something about ‘the machine knows what it’s been through.’” Elias laughed, but it didn’t feel like a joke. He didn’t know what that meant, but he remembered the old purifier had a small brass plate with a name engraved on it—Sylvia, from 1983. And Sylvia had been the one who first tried to grow mutfruit in this area.

So he went back to the Memory Den and asked the caretaker if they had any records of the purifier’s history. The man in the dim light behind a stack of salvaged radios said he didn’t, but he did have a sketch of the original setup. It showed the copper coil wrapped in cloth, like a wound, and a note in pencil: “Don’t let it go dry. It remembers.” Elias didn’t believe in ghosts, but he did believe in the soil remembering what it had lost. So he used the old pump, rigged it with salvaged tubing, and ran it through the old filter. It didn’t work perfectly—but it kept the water from flooding the mutfruit beds.

By morning, the first shoots had cracked through the soil. Lila came back with a smile and said she’d seen a new patch of wild clover near the third rail. “Looks like something’s growing again,” she said. “Even if it’s just a little.” Elias didn’t say anything, just nodded and fed a handful of mutfruit to the brahmin at the gate. It chewed slowly, eyes half-lidded, like it was tasting something it hadn’t seen in years. And for the first time in months, the soil didn’t feel like it was dying. It felt like it was breathing.
